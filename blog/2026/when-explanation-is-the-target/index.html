When the Explanation Is the Target: Building a Reproducible XAI Robustness Benchmark

Created on October 19, 2026 by Clifford

2026   ·   trustworthy ai   explainable ai   cybersecurity   research

An explanation can make an AI decision easier to inspect. It can also create a false sense of security.

That tension shaped my MSc research into explainable AI for intrusion detection. When a system flags network activity as suspicious, a security analyst may look at an explanation to understand which features influenced the prediction. But what if the model’s prediction stays the same while the explanation changes? An analyst could be shown a convincing story about a decision without a dependable way to know whether that story is stable.

My research asked a focused question: can post-hoc explanations remain dependable when an attacker can influence the input or deliberately redirect the explanation?

This article explains the benchmark I built to investigate that question and summarises what it showed. The dissertation has been graded and formally accepted. It has not been peer reviewed or formally published, so every result below holds only for the conditions I tested.

Explanations are an attack surface too

Intrusion detection systems classify network activity as benign or malicious. Machine-learning versions of these systems can recognise patterns across many traffic features, but their decisions are not always obvious to the analyst who has to investigate an alert.

Post-hoc explainers such as SHAP and LIME try to make a single prediction more legible. They assign an importance value to each feature, helping an analyst inspect what the fitted model associated with that decision. That can be useful, but it creates a second object that deserves evaluation: the explanation itself.

I did not treat explanation robustness as one broad property. The benchmark separates three questions that are often blended together:

  1. Is the underlying detector good enough to evaluate? A weak or unstable classifier makes a robustness result difficult to interpret.
  2. Does an explanation change under a bounded input perturbation? This is explanation fragility: the explanation may shift as the input changes, whether or not the prediction is preserved.
  3. Can an explanation be deliberately redirected while the prediction is held fixed? This is targeted tampering. The decision remains the same, but the explanation presented to the analyst is pushed away from the feature it originally emphasised.

The distinction matters. An explanation that is sensitive to ordinary perturbation is not automatically easy to manipulate in a targeted way, and a stable-looking explanation is not automatically reliable.

A benchmark, not a striking example

It is easy to demonstrate one unexpected explanation. It is much harder to create an experiment where another person can see exactly what was varied, what was held fixed, and how the evidence was produced.

I therefore built a configuration-driven benchmarking framework around two established public intrusion-detection datasets: CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15. Both contain labelled records of benign and malicious network traffic, but they have different feature schemas, traffic sources, and attack categories. Using both does not make the results universal; it provides a bounded check across two distinct experimental settings.

The framework evaluates two common model families: XGBoost, a tree ensemble, and a multi-layer perceptron. It then applies two widely used post-hoc feature-attribution methods with different mechanisms: TreeSHAP, an exact SHAP algorithm for tree models, on XGBoost; and LIME, a sampling-based method, on both models. The goal is not to crown a universally safer model or explainer. It is to test specified model–explainer pairings under the same documented conditions.

This choice shapes how the results can be read. LIME can support a bounded, architecture-associated comparison because the same explainer is used with both models. TreeSHAP is specific to the tree model. A model-agnostic SHAP approximation was evaluated separately as a reliability control, not used to turn the SHAP comparison into a cleaner architecture claim.

flowchart TD
  S[Define scope and interpretation rules] --> D[Prepare public IDS datasets]
  D --> M[Train detector models]
  M --> G1{Model-quality gate}
  G1 -->|passes| E[Generate baseline explanations]
  G1 -->|fails| A[Audit record: not primary evidence]
  E --> G2{Explainer-reliability gate}
  G2 -->|passes| F[Fragility strand: bounded input perturbations]
  G2 -->|passes| T[Tamper strand: move explanation, preserve prediction]
  G2 -->|fails| A
  F --> V[Evaluate stability, drift, fidelity, and tamper criteria]
  T --> V
  V --> P[Store validated, traceable evidence]

The diagram is deliberately more important than it looks. The two gates stop a poor detector or unreliable explainer from silently becoming evidence about robustness. A failed configuration remains an audit record; it is not quietly deleted or allowed into the primary analysis. An experiment should not answer a sophisticated question with a measurement tool that has not first earned that role.

Two related, but different, attack strands

The first strand measures fragility. It applies bounded, loss-directed perturbations inspired by fast-gradient and projected-gradient approaches. The aim is to observe how the explanation responds to a controlled input change. A prediction may change in this strand; prediction retention is recorded as a diagnostic rather than used to filter inconvenient cases away.

The second strand measures tamper resistance. Here the optimisation target is not simply the detector’s loss. It is the explanation. The benchmark tries to reduce the apparent importance of the feature that the baseline explanation ranked highest, while preserving the detector’s prediction.

This gives the research a concrete analyst-facing interpretation: could a decision remain “malicious” while the explanation no longer draws attention to the characteristic that originally appeared most important?

A deliberately bounded threat model

The threat model is query-based. It assumes that an attacker can submit a modified input, observe the detector’s probability output, and obtain an explanation for that input. It does not assume access to model weights, gradients, training data, architecture, or explainer internals.

Access to the explanation interface is an explicit experimental assumption, not a claim about every deployed intrusion-detection system. In practice, feasibility would also depend on which interfaces are exposed, authentication, rate limits, monitoring, and the difficulty of introducing a modified network flow.

This boundary also keeps the comparison fair. The tree model and neural network have different internal structures, but they share the same external experimental interface. A gradient-free optimisation method, simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation (SPSA), lets the benchmark apply one consistent attack formulation across both model families without silently giving one of them a more powerful adversary.

Reproducibility is part of the method

For this research, reproducibility was not an appendix task. It shaped the framework itself.

The data pipeline fits preprocessing transformations on training data only, then applies them unchanged to held-out test data. It removes identifiers and shortcut-prone fields that could make an intrusion detector appear stronger for the wrong reason. The test cohort used for attack evaluation is frozen with a documented random seed, so later stages are comparing the same records rather than a moving sample.

Each experiment is defined through configuration rather than hidden notebook state. A validated configuration specifies the dataset, model, explainer, attack, metrics, seed, and output location. This gives each run a clear contract before data is loaded or a model is trained.

The implementation follows the same separation of concerns. A runner coordinates data preparation, model training, explanation extraction, attacks, and metrics. A component registry lets a named dataset, model, explainer, attack, or metric be selected through that configuration. The resulting record captures the resolved settings, seed, artifacts, and provenance needed to reproduce or audit a run. Automated checks validate configurations, preprocessing, model outputs, explanations, attacks, metrics, and result structure.

That discipline became especially important for explanation methods that include sampling or approximation. A result cannot be called robust merely because one run appears stable. The benchmark therefore distinguishes a method’s own benign variation from changes associated with the attack condition.

What the benchmark measures

The evaluation does not rely on a single visualisation or a single score. It uses complementary checks:

  • Rank stability: whether influential features remain similarly ranked.
  • Attribution drift: how far the explanation vector moves.
  • Fidelity: whether a reported attribution remains meaningfully connected to the model behaviour under a defined intervention. I treat this as an auxiliary check, because deleting or inserting features can itself create unrealistic inputs.
  • Tamper criteria: whether a targeted feature has been displaced while the model decision remains intact.

These metrics answer different questions. A familiar-looking top feature list does not guarantee that attribution magnitudes are stable. A changed attribution does not prove a decision has changed. And a method that varies because of its own approximation process needs a different interpretation from one that varies because an input was attacked.

For targeted tampering, the benchmark separates the attack objective from the assessment. The attack tries to suppress the feature the baseline explanation ranked highest. The assessment then checks whether that feature was actually displaced while the prediction remained fixed, alongside the broader stability and fidelity measures. This makes it harder to confuse optimising one score with demonstrating a meaningful explanation change.

What the benchmark found, within the tested conditions

The final evidence base is 126 validated, condition-level results: two datasets, three model–explainer pairings, two attack strands, and three repeatability seeds. Every included detector met the quality gate, with attack-class recall and F1 of at least 0.80.

  • TreeSHAP explanations were highly fragile. Under the standard perturbations, mean cosine drift between clean and attacked explanations was 0.95 on CICIDS2017 and 0.72 on UNSW-NB15. On this scale, 0 means no change and a value near 1 means the explanation became unrelated to the original.
  • LIME looked stable, but that is not evidence of robustness. Its raw drift was small. However, in none of the 72 LIME fragility conditions was the attack drift distinguishable from LIME’s own run-to-run noise. Any attack effect, if present, could not be separated from the method’s sampling variation.
  • LIME stability differed by model. With LIME held fixed, feature rankings were more stable on the neural network than on XGBoost. This is an association within the tested conditions, not a causal claim about architectures.
  • Targeted tampering worked, but not everywhere. For TreeSHAP on UNSW-NB15, the attack pushed the originally most important feature out of the top ten in 20.3% of flows (95% CI 18.3–22.3%), while the prediction stayed the same. On CICIDS2017, the same pairing barely moved (0.1%). LIME showed some movement in the tamper runs, but again it could not be separated from LIME’s own noise.

That last contrast is the most useful one. On CICIDS2017, the ordinary perturbations flipped almost every prediction and moved the TreeSHAP explanations a great deal. Yet the targeted attack, which had to keep the prediction fixed, barely moved them. Fragility and tamperability are not the same property.

The reliability gate also did its job. I tested KernelSHAP, a model-agnostic approximation of SHAP, as a cross-check on the TreeSHAP results. It passed a small calibration run but failed the reliability gate at full scale, with 500 flows per run: seven of nine completed runs exceeded the pre-set limit on drift between repeated explanations of the same input. I did not relax the limit after seeing the result. KernelSHAP is reported as an explainer-reliability finding, not as robustness evidence, and the SHAP results stay confined to TreeSHAP on XGBoost.

The central result is that three properties need separate tests: whether an explainer is numerically reliable, how fragile its explanations are, and how easily they can be deliberately redirected. Passing one test says little about the others.

Deliberate limits

This is a controlled robustness experiment, not a simulation of every real network adversary. The perturbations are experimental stimuli, not claims about physically realisable network flows. The threat model assumes the attacker can query the explanation, which not every deployment allows. The perturbation budget is defined in normalised feature space, so it is comparable within a dataset but not directly across datasets. The research is limited to the public datasets, the tested model–explainer pairings, the defined budgets, and the evaluation choices in the benchmark.

Those limits are not a weakness to hide. They describe the boundary inside which the experiment can make a defensible contribution. The aim is to make claims that are precise enough to test, revise, and reproduce—not to make explainable AI look settled before it is.

The wider lesson

The work has changed how I think about explainability in applied AI. An explanation should not be treated as a decorative layer added after model validation. It is part of the system a person may rely on, and it needs its own reliability checks, threat model, and evidence trail.

That is also why this research connects naturally to my engineering work. In a data platform or AI product, trust is not created by displaying more detail. It comes from being explicit about what a system can support, how that support was tested, and where the evidence stops.
